Exercise and Blood Sugar — Moving Safely with Diabetes

How exercise affects blood sugar, the best activities, and how to exercise safely with diabetes.

Why exercise helps

Exercise is one of the most powerful tools for managing diabetes. It helps muscles use sugar from the blood, which lowers blood sugar during and after activity, and it improves insulin sensitivity for hours afterwards. Regular exercise also helps weight, blood pressure, cholesterol, mood, and sleep, all of which matter for diabetes. People who exercise regularly often need less diabetes medicine, though doses should only change with your care team's advice. The benefits start with the first walk and grow with consistency.

How exercise affects blood sugar

Most exercise lowers blood sugar, but the effect depends on the type, intensity, and timing. Moderate aerobic exercise such as brisk walking, cycling, or swimming usually lowers blood sugar during and after the session. Very intense exercise can raise blood sugar briefly, because stress hormones release stored sugar. The overall effect over weeks is better control, but individual sessions vary, so checking your blood sugar before, during, and after exercise, especially when starting, teaches you how your body responds. Keep a short record of activity and readings to share with your care team.

Best activities

Walking is the best starting point: it is safe, free, and effective, and walking after meals is especially helpful for blunting the blood sugar rise. Aim to build up to 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as 30 minutes of brisk walking on five days. Strength training twice a week preserves muscle and improves insulin sensitivity; bodyweight exercises, bands, or light weights are enough. Swimming, cycling, and dancing are good alternatives if walking is difficult. Choose activities you enjoy, because enjoyment predicts whether you will keep doing them.

Safety and low blood sugar

If you take insulin or certain diabetes tablets, exercise can cause low blood sugar, known as a hypo. Check your blood sugar before exercising, carry glucose tablets or your usual hypo treatment, and do not exercise alone in a remote place when starting out. Eat a small carbohydrate snack before exercise if your blood sugar is low or if your care team advises it. Avoid exercising when your blood sugar is very high and you have ketones, which your care team can explain. Wear well-fitting shoes and check your feet after exercise, because diabetes can reduce feeling in the feet and slow healing.

Foot care and other precautions

Foot care deserves attention: diabetes can damage nerves and blood vessels in the feet, so blisters and cuts can become serious. Wear clean, well-fitting socks and shoes, check your feet daily, and see a professional promptly for any sore that does not heal. Stay hydrated, especially in hot weather, and wear sunscreen. If you have eye, kidney, or heart complications, ask your care team which activities are safe for you. Start slowly and build up over weeks; a ten-minute walk is a good first step, and adding five minutes a week is a sensible pace.

Making it a habit

Schedule exercise like an appointment, walk at the same time each day, and pair it with something you enjoy, such as a podcast or a friend. Track your activity and your blood sugar together for a month, so you can see the benefit. If you miss a session, do the next one rather than waiting for Monday. Exercise is not a punishment for eating; it is a tool that makes blood sugar easier to manage and life more enjoyable. Even small amounts help, and the people who benefit most are those who keep moving, week after week, for years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does exercise lower blood sugar?

Yes, usually. Moderate exercise such as brisk walking helps muscles use sugar from the blood and improves insulin sensitivity for hours afterwards. Very intense exercise can raise blood sugar briefly.

Can I exercise if I take insulin?

Yes, with care. Check your blood sugar before, during, and after exercise, carry hypo treatment, and discuss your plan with your care team, as insulin doses may need adjusting.

How much exercise should I do?

Aim for 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as 30 minutes of brisk walking on five days, plus strength training twice a week. Build up gradually from where you are now.
