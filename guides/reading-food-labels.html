Reading Food Labels — A Guide for Diabetes

How to read nutrition labels for diabetes: carbohydrate, sugars, fibre, fat, salt, and the claims that mislead.

Why labels matter

Food labels are the most reliable source of information about what you are eating, and for diabetes the carbohydrate figure is the most important number on the packet. Learning to read labels takes a few minutes and pays off every time you shop, because it lets you compare products, spot hidden sugar, and count carbohydrate accurately. This guide explains what to look for, in order, and which claims to ignore.

Carbohydrate first

Find the nutrition table and look for carbohydrate, given per 100 grams and per serving. The per-serving figure is the one to use, but check that the serving size matches what you actually eat. The line for sugars is part of total carbohydrate, not in addition to it, so do not add the two together. Fibre is also listed within total carbohydrate on most labels. Use the per-100-gram column to compare products fairly, because serving sizes differ between brands. A product with 10 grams of carbohydrate per 100 grams is lower in carbohydrate than one with 40 grams, whatever the serving size says.

Sugars and sweeteners

The sugars line shows naturally occurring and added sugars together. A yoghurt may show 5 grams of sugars from milk, while a flavoured yoghurt shows 15 grams because sugar was added. The ingredients list shows the source: sugar, honey, syrup, dextrose, and fruit juice concentrate are all added sugars. Ingredients are listed by weight, so if sugar is one of the first three ingredients, the product is high in sugar. Sweeteners such as sucralose, aspartame, and stevia do not raise blood sugar, but a product containing them can still be high in carbohydrate from flour or starch.

Fat, salt, and fibre

Check total fat and saturated fat. Saturated fat should be low, because it raises heart risk, which is already higher with diabetes. Choose products with unsaturated fats where possible. Salt is listed as salt or sodium; aim for products with less than 0.3 grams of salt per 100 grams where you can. Fibre is listed separately in many countries; higher is better, and 3 grams or more per serving is a good amount. A product high in fibre and low in sugar is usually a better choice than one low in both.

Claims that mislead

Claims on the front of a packet are marketing, not nutrition. Sugar-free does not mean low in carbohydrate. Low-fat often means high in sugar, because sugar replaces the fat. No added sugar does not mean sugar-free, because the product may contain fruit sugars. Natural does not mean healthy, and wholegrain does not mean high in fibre unless the label says so. Always turn the packet over and read the nutrition table and ingredients, because the front is advertising and the back is information.

Putting it into practice

Practise with foods you buy often. Compare two breads, two cereals, and two yoghurts using the per-100-gram column, and choose the one with more fibre and less sugar. Take a photo of labels you find confusing and ask your dietitian at your next appointment. After a few weeks, label reading becomes quick and automatic, and your shopping trolley changes without much effort. Labels are a tool, not a test; use them to make better choices most of the time, not to achieve perfection.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most important number on a label for diabetes?

Total carbohydrate, per serving. The sugars line is part of total carbohydrate, not in addition to it. Use the per-100-gram column to compare products fairly.

Does sugar-free mean low in carbohydrate?

No. Sugar-free products can still contain a lot of carbohydrate from flour, starch, or sugar alcohols. Always check total carbohydrate on the label.

How do I spot hidden sugar on a label?

Check the ingredients list: sugar, honey, syrup, dextrose, maltose, and fruit juice concentrate are all added sugars. Ingredients are listed by weight, so sugar near the top means a lot of it.
