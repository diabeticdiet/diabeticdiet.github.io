Low-GI Foods — A Practical Guide to the Glycemic Index

What glycemic index means, which foods are low GI, and how to use GI without overcomplicating your meals.

What glycemic index means

The glycemic index, or GI, ranks carbohydrate foods by how quickly they raise blood sugar compared with pure glucose, which scores 100. Low GI is under 55, medium is 56 to 69, and high is 70 or above. Low-GI foods are digested more slowly, so blood sugar rises more gently and stays steadier. High-GI foods are digested fast and cause a sharper rise. GI is a useful guide, but it is not the whole story: the amount of carbohydrate you eat matters too, and so does what you eat with it. A small portion of a high-GI food may raise blood sugar less than a large portion of a low-GI one.

Reliable low-GI foods

Many everyday foods are naturally low GI. These include rolled oats, barley, lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans, most vegetables, most whole fruits such as apples, pears, oranges, and berries, wholemeal bread, pasta cooked al dente, sweet potato, milk, and yoghurt. These foods are also rich in fibre, vitamins, and minerals, so they are good choices for general health as well as blood sugar. Building meals around them is one of the simplest ways to improve blood sugar control without feeling restricted. Medium and high-GI foods

Medium-GI foods include brown rice, couscous, honey, and some breakfast cereals. High-GI foods include white bread, jasmine rice, baked potatoes, cornflakes, and many processed snacks. These foods are not banned, but portions matter more with them, and pairing them with protein, fat, or fibre helps. For example, white rice eaten with vegetables, fish, and a little oil raises blood sugar more slowly than white rice eaten alone. Cooking also matters: pasta cooked al dente has a lower GI than overcooked pasta, and cooling cooked potatoes or rice and reheating them can lower their GI slightly by forming resistant starch.

Glycemic load: the missing half

GI tells you about speed, but glycemic load tells you about speed and amount together. Watermelon has a high GI of about 72, but a typical serving contains little carbohydrate, so its glycemic load is low. That is why watermelon in a normal portion is usually fine. To judge a food, consider both: a high-GI food in a small portion may be manageable, while a low-GI food in a very large portion can still raise blood sugar a lot. Portion awareness and GI work together, and neither replaces the other.

Using GI in real meals

Use GI as a swap guide rather than a rulebook. Swap white bread for wholemeal, jasmine rice for brown rice or barley, cornflakes for porridge, and instant mashed potato for sweet potato. Add lentils or beans to soups and stews. Choose whole fruit instead of juice. Keep the rest of the meal balanced with protein and vegetables. These swaps are small, but across a week they add up to steadier blood sugar and better fullness. Do not try to memorise every GI value; learn the pattern for the foods you eat most often, and check new ones as they come up.

What GI does not do

GI does not measure nutrition. Chocolate has a medium GI, and chips have a moderate GI, but neither is a healthy food. GI also does not account for how much you eat, and values vary with variety, ripeness, and cooking method. A green banana has a lower GI than a very ripe one. So use GI alongside other guides: choose whole, minimally processed foods, watch portions, and keep meals balanced. If you take insulin or other diabetes medicines, discuss GI-based changes with your care team, because slower carbohydrate can change the timing of your blood sugar response.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is GI the same as sugar content?

No. GI measures how quickly a food raises blood sugar, not how much sugar it contains. Some sugary foods have a medium GI, and some starchy foods with no added sugar have a high GI.

Are all low-GI foods healthy?

No. Some low-GI foods, such as chocolate, are high in fat and calories. Use GI together with overall nutrition — whole foods, fibre, and balanced portions — not on its own.

Does cooking change a food's GI?

Yes. Longer cooking usually raises GI, because starch breaks down more. Pasta cooked al dente has a lower GI than soft pasta, and cooling and reheating starchy foods can lower GI slightly.
