Carb Counting — A Step-by-Step Guide for Diabetes

How to count carbohydrates accurately: labels, portions, common foods, and the mistakes that cause most errors.

Why count carbs

Carbohydrate counting means estimating how many grams of carbohydrate you eat at each meal and snack. For people with diabetes, it is one of the most useful skills, because carbohydrate has the largest effect on blood sugar. Counting helps you match food to your medicine, keep portions consistent, and spot which meals cause the biggest rises. You do not need to count perfectly; a consistent, honest estimate is far more useful than a precise one you abandon after a week. Many people find that two weeks of careful counting teaches them enough to judge portions by eye for months afterwards.

Reading food labels

Start with the nutrition label, which lists carbohydrate per 100 grams and per serving. The per-serving figure is the one to use, but check that the serving size on the label matches what you actually eat. The line for sugars is part of the total carbohydrate, not in addition to it, so do not add the two together. Fibre is also listed within total carbohydrate in most countries, and some people subtract fibre, but follow your care team's advice on that. Weighing food for a short learning period makes label maths much more accurate, because a guess of 80 grams of rice can easily be 150 grams.

Common foods and their carbs

Learning the carbohydrate content of foods you eat often is the fastest route to confident counting. A slice of bread is about 15 grams, a medium apple about 15 grams, a cup of milk about 12 grams, two-thirds of a cup of cooked rice about 30 grams, and a medium boiled potato about 20 grams. Our carb counter on the home page lists these and more, and lets you multiply by servings. Keep a short personal list of your own frequent foods with their values, and update it when you try something new. Restaurant meals are harder, so estimate generously and check your blood sugar afterwards to learn from the result.

Counting mixed meals

Mixed dishes are the hardest part of counting, because several carbohydrate foods are combined. Break the meal into its parts: the rice or bread, the sauce, the vegetables, and any fruit or dessert. Estimate each part, then add them up. A curry with rice, for example, is the rice plus the sauce's carbohydrate plus any bread. Soups with potatoes or lentils need an estimate too. If a meal is new, write down your estimate and your blood sugar before and after eating; that feedback teaches you more than any table. Over time, you will build a reliable sense of your regular meals.

Tools and practice

A kitchen scale, a set of measuring cups, and a notebook or phone note are the basic tools. Weigh food for two weeks, measure drinks, and record your estimates alongside your blood sugar readings. Phone apps can help, but check their figures against labels, because app databases vary in accuracy. Practise with packaged foods first, where the label gives the answer, then move to home-cooked meals. Counting gets faster with practice; most people find it takes a few minutes per meal at first and under a minute once the common foods are familiar.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common errors are forgetting drinks, sauces, and snacks; confusing the per-100-gram figure with the per-serving figure; and underestimating restaurant portions. Sauces can contain surprising amounts of sugar, and a large takeaway portion can be double a home portion. Another mistake is counting only at main meals and ignoring grazing between meals, which can add a whole meal's worth of carbohydrate over a day. Counting is a skill, not a test. If a meal's estimate was wrong, note it, learn from it, and move on rather than feeling you have failed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to count carbs at every meal?

Counting at every meal is most useful when you are learning, or when you use insulin. Once you know your regular meals, many people count carefully at main meals and estimate snacks. Ask your care team what suits you.

Is fibre counted as carbohydrate?

Fibre is listed as part of total carbohydrate on most labels. Whether you subtract it depends on your care team's advice and local guidance, so follow their method consistently.

How accurate does carb counting need to be?

Within about 10 grams per meal is a good aim. Small errors are normal and your body tolerates them; the goal is consistency, not perfection.
