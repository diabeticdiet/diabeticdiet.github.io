Diabetes and Alcohol — What You Need to Know

How alcohol affects blood sugar, the risks for people with diabetes, and how to drink more safely if you choose to.

How alcohol affects blood sugar

Alcohol has a complicated effect on blood sugar. At first, sugary drinks such as beer, sweet wine, and cocktails can raise blood sugar, because they contain carbohydrate. But alcohol itself lowers blood sugar, because the liver is busy processing alcohol and cannot release stored sugar as it normally would. This lowering effect can last for many hours, even overnight, and it can cause low blood sugar, known as a hypo, especially in people who take insulin or certain diabetes tablets. This delayed effect is the main danger, and it is why alcohol needs care.

The risk of low blood sugar

The risk is highest when alcohol is drunk on an empty stomach, in large amounts, or before bed. Symptoms of low blood sugar, such as confusion, sweating, and shakiness, can be mistaken for drunkenness, which makes the situation more dangerous. If you take insulin or tablets that can cause hypos, always eat when you drink, keep your hypo treatment with you, and tell someone you are with that you have diabetes. Check your blood sugar before bed after drinking, and eat a snack containing carbohydrate if your care team advises it. Never drink alone if you are at risk of a hypo.

Alcohol and calories

Alcohol is high in calories, about 7 calories per gram, nearly as much as fat, and it adds calories without nutrition. A pint of beer has about 180 calories, a large glass of wine about 190, and a cocktail can have 300 or more. Alcohol also lowers inhibitions around food, so people often eat more after drinking, and it disrupts sleep, which worsens hunger the next day. Cutting back on alcohol is one of the easiest ways to reduce calorie intake and improve blood sugar control.

Which drinks are better

If you choose to drink, drier options are usually better than sweet ones. Dry wine and spirits with a sugar-free mixer contain less carbohydrate than beer, sweet wine, or cocktails, though all alcohol carries the hypo risk. Keep servings small, alternate alcoholic drinks with water, and avoid sugary mixers and alcopops. A small glass of wine with a meal is a lower-risk choice than several drinks on an empty stomach. There is no completely safe drink for diabetes; the risk depends on the amount, the food eaten with it, and your medicine.

Limits and safer drinking

General guidance is to keep alcohol within recommended limits, currently 14 units a week in the UK, spread over several days, with alcohol-free days each week. A unit is about half a pint of beer or a small glass of wine. If you have diabetes, discuss alcohol with your care team, because your personal limit may be lower. Avoid drinking every day, avoid binge drinking, and never drink on an empty stomach. If you have liver, nerve, or eye complications, your care team may advise you not to drink at all.

When not to drink

Do not drink if you are pregnant, if your blood sugar is poorly controlled, if you have a history of severe hypos, or if your care team has advised against it. Do not drink before driving or operating machinery. If you find it hard to cut back, talk to your doctor, because support is available and asking for it is a sign of strength. Alcohol is a personal choice, and many people with diabetes choose not to drink at all. Whatever you decide, make it an informed choice, and keep your care team involved.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drink alcohol with diabetes?

Many people can, in moderation and with food, but alcohol can cause delayed low blood sugar, especially if you take insulin or certain tablets. Discuss it with your care team.

Why does alcohol lower blood sugar?

The liver is busy processing alcohol and cannot release stored sugar, so blood sugar can fall for many hours after drinking, even overnight. Eating with alcohol reduces this risk.

What is the safest alcoholic drink for diabetes?

No drink is completely safe. Dry wine or spirits with a sugar-free mixer contain less carbohydrate than beer or cocktails, but the hypo risk remains. Keep servings small and always eat when drinking.
